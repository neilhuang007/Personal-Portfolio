About Me

"Life is like a box of chocolates. You never know what you're gonna get."

Forrest Gump

Hi, I'm Neil, a programming enthusiast since childhood. I started programming when I was seven, and ever since then I've been fascinated with the art of solving problems. Programming gave me a way to modify the world around me and solve problems that had occupied my mind for a long time. Being able to reason through a difficult problem and come up with an interesting solution feels more rewarding than almost anything else in the world. Throughout my journey, I've mostly learned as I go, acquiring knowledge whenever I needed it, which, surprisingly, has turned out to be extremely important in the fields of mathematics and science.

Algorithmic thinking changed my life. It allowed me to approach things from first principles and, over time, develop and refine my own methodology for completing everyday tasks. This has become especially important in the age of AI, where the productivity of an agent depends not only on its capabilities, but also on the tasks it is given and the thinking of the engineer behind it. I often ask myself, in a world where AI agents can write all the code for us, what is left for a human to do? My answer to that is design. In a sense, we've been doing this all along, though at a much higher cost. Code has never been the real bottleneck for a great product or system. Thinking is. Programmers who can build features are not hard to find, but the real breakthrough lies in knowing what to build, how to design the architecture, and why the system should exist in the first place. For me, AI has begun to take implementation out of the conversation, leaving us with what has always mattered most: design.

Outside of programming, I am also an artist. I even studied art at the Rhode Island School of Design! The reason I love art is that I believe it awakens a different part of the mind. It allows us to embrace the unrealized instincts and the sense of appreciation we are born with, things we may feel deeply without ever fully understanding. There is abstract art that cannot be quantified or explained, and beauty cannot always be reduced to data. Sometimes a certain artistic choice makes a work more brilliant, yet no amount of reasoning can precisely explain why. If an artist creates her best work during a time of strong emotion, does that make her any less of an artist at other times? Perhaps this is why AI agents still lack human taste (laugh).

What connects art and computer science for me is my passion for anthropology. I love traveling to different parts of the world, and each place adds something to my personality, as though a part of it becomes permanently frozen in my memory. I've made numerous attempts to understand the mechanisms behind certain social phenomena, to see why people behave the way they do, how cultures take shape, and how much of it can be quantified. There is something fascinating about trying to understand the human world through reason, while knowing that some parts of it may never be fully explained. But I'd rather share less here and leave the rest of my biggest passion as a gift for those who meet me in real life.
