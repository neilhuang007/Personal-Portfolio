# Computer science bookshelf

## 01. The Art of Computer Programming
Donald E. Knuth
Volume 1: Fundamental Algorithms
Preface

> The process of preparing programs for a digital
> computer is especially attractive, not only
> because it can be economically and scientifically
> rewarding, but also because it can be an
> aesthetic experience much like composing poetry
> or music. This book is the first volume of a
> multi-volume set of books that has been designed
> to train the reader in various skills that go
> into a programmer’s craft.

> The following chapters are not meant to serve
> as an introduction to computer programming; the
> reader is supposed to have had some previous
> experience. The prerequisites are actually very
> simple, but a beginner requires time and practice
> in order to understand the concept of a digital
> computer. The reader should possess:

> a) Some idea of how a stored-program digital
>    computer works; not necessarily the
>    electronics, rather the manner in which
>    instructions can be kept in the machine’s
>    memory and successively executed.

> b) An ability to put the solutions to problems
>    into such explicit terms that a computer can
>    “understand” them. (These machines have no
>    common sense; they do exactly as they are
>    told, no more and no less. This fact is the
>    hardest concept to grasp when one first tries
>    to use a computer.)

> c) Some knowledge of the most elementary computer
>    techniques, such as looping (performing a set
>    of instructions repeatedly), the use of
>    subroutines, and the use of indexed variables.

> d) A little knowledge of common computer jargon —
>    “memory,” “registers,” “bits,” “floating point,”
>    “overflow,” “software.” Most words not defined
>    in the text are given brief definitions in the
>    index at the close of each volume.

> These four prerequisites can perhaps be summed
> up into the single requirement that the reader
> should have already written and tested at least,
> say, four programs for at least one computer.

> I have tried to write this set of books in such
> a way that it will fill several needs. In the
> first place, these books are reference works
> that summarize the knowledge that has been
> acquired in several important fields. In the
> second place, they can be used as textbooks for
> self-study or for college courses in the computer
> and information sciences. To meet both of these
> objectives, I have incorporated a large number
> of exercises into the text and have furnished
> answers for most of them. I have also made an
> effort to fill the pages with facts rather than
> with vague, general commentary.

[Source](https://www.informit.com/store/art-of-computer-programming-volume-1-fundamental-algorithms-9780201896831)

## 06. Clean Code
Robert C. Martin
Introduction

> Which door represents your code? Which door
> represents your team or your company?

> Why are we in that room? Is this just a normal
> code review or have we found a stream of horrible
> problems shortly after going live? Are we
> debugging in a panic, poring over code that we
> thought worked? Are customers leaving in droves
> and managers breathing down our necks? How can
> we make sure we wind up behind the right door
> when the going gets tough? The answer is:
> craftsmanship.

> There are two parts to learning craftsmanship:
> knowledge and work. You must gain the knowledge
> of principles, patterns, practices, and
> heuristics that a craftsman knows, and you must
> also grind that knowledge into your fingers,
> eyes, and gut by working hard and practicing.

> I can teach you the physics of riding a bicycle.
> Indeed, the classical mathematics is relatively
> straightforward. Gravity, friction, angular
> momentum, center of mass, and so forth, can be
> demonstrated with less than a page full of
> equations. Given those formulae I could prove
> to you that bicycle riding is practical and
> give you all the knowledge you needed to make
> it work. And you’d still fall down the first
> time you climbed on that bike.

> Coding is no different. We could write down all
> the “feel good” principles of clean code and
> then trust you to do the work (in other words,
> let you fall down when you get on the bike),
> but then what kind of teachers would that make
> us, and what kind of student would that make you?

> No. That’s not the way this book is going to work.

[Source](https://www.informit.com/content/images/9780132350884/samplepages/9780132350884.pdf)

About Me

"Life is like a box of chocolates. You never know what you're gonna get."

Forrest Gump

Hi, I'm Neil, a programming enthusiast since childhood. I started programming when I was seven, and ever since then I've been fascinated with the art of solving problems. Programming gave me a way to modify the world around me and solve problems that had occupied my mind for a long time. Being able to reason through a difficult problem and come up with an interesting solution feels more rewarding than almost anything else in the world. Throughout my journey, I've mostly learned as I go, acquiring knowledge whenever I needed it, which, surprisingly, has turned out to be extremely important in the fields of mathematics and science.

Algorithmic thinking changed my life. It allowed me to approach things from first principles and, over time, develop and refine my own methodology for completing everyday tasks. This has become especially important in the age of AI, where the productivity of an agent depends not only on its capabilities, but also on the tasks it is given and the thinking of the engineer behind it. I often ask myself, in a world where AI agents can write all the code for us, what is left for a human to do? My answer to that is design. In a sense, we've been doing this all along, though at a much higher cost. Code has never been the real bottleneck for a great product or system. Thinking is. Programmers who can build features are not hard to find, but the real breakthrough lies in knowing what to build, how to design the architecture, and why the system should exist in the first place. For me, AI has begun to take implementation out of the conversation, leaving us with what has always mattered most: design.

Outside of programming, I am also an artist. I even studied art at the Rhode Island School of Design! The reason I love art is that I believe it awakens a different part of the mind. It allows us to embrace the unrealized instincts and the sense of appreciation we are born with, things we may feel deeply without ever fully understanding. There is abstract art that cannot be quantified or explained, and beauty cannot always be reduced to data. Sometimes a certain artistic choice makes a work more brilliant, yet no amount of reasoning can precisely explain why. If an artist creates her best work during a time of strong emotion, does that make her any less of an artist at other times? Perhaps this is why AI agents still lack human taste (laugh).

What connects art and computer science for me is my passion for anthropology. I love traveling to different parts of the world, and each place adds something to my personality, as though a part of it becomes permanently frozen in my memory. I've made numerous attempts to understand the mechanisms behind certain social phenomena, to see why people behave the way they do, how cultures take shape, and how much of it can be quantified. There is something fascinating about trying to understand the human world through reason, while knowing that some parts of it may never be fully explained. But I'd rather share less here and leave the rest of my biggest passion as a gift for those who meet me in real life.
